Image Quality Assessment: Exploring Joint
Degradation Effect of Deep Network Features via
Kernel Representation Similarity Analysis
Xingran Liao , Xuekai Wei , Mingliang Zhou , Senior Member, IEEE, Hau-San Wong , Member, IEEE,
and Sam Kwong , Fellow, IEEE

Abstract—Typically, deep network-based full-reference image
quality assessment (FR-IQA) models compare deep features from
reference and distorted images pairwise, overlooking correlations
among features from the same source. We propose a dual-branch
framework to capture the joint degradation effect among deep
network features. The first branch uses kernel representation similarity analysis (KRSA), which compares feature self-similarity
matrices via the mean absolute error (MAE). The second branch
conducts pairwise comparisons via the MAE, and a training-free
logarithmic summation of both branches derives the final score.
Our approach contributes in three ways. First, integrating the
KRSA with pairwise comparisons enhances the model’s perceptual awareness. Second, our approach is adaptable to diverse
network architectures. Third, our approach can guide perceptual
image enhancement. Extensive experiments on 10 datasets validate
our method’s efficacy, demonstrating that perceptual deformation
widely exists in diverse IQA scenarios and that measuring the joint
degradation effect can discern appealing content deformations.
Index Terms—Image quality assessment, kernel representation
similarity analysis, perceptual evaluation, deep neural network.

scores are fused through training-free logarithmic summation.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Various Quality Assessment Scenarios
The development of FR-IQA models has never detached
from practical quality assessment scenarios. Classic FR-IQA
databases, including the TID2013 [10], Laboratory for Image
& Video Engineering (LIVE) [19], Categorical Image Quality
(CSIQ) [20], and KADID-10k [11], seek to explore HVS behaviour when confronted with information loss during image
transmissions. These datasets contain images affected by diverse types of synthetic distortion, which are generated within
a controlled laboratory environment. Subjective scores are assigned to these images via the Swiss scoring system, whose
range usually lies within a fixed interval. Recent research has
emphasized quality assessment issues on images generated by
various image processing algorithms. The PIPAL dataset [12] involves distortion types generated by modern image enhancement
algorithms, such as GAN-based image denoising and superresolution. The quality assessment database for superresolution algorithms (QADS) [21] mainly contains images from diverse superresolution algorithms. The synthesis texture quality database
(SynTEX) [22] and the texture quality database (TQD) [1]
focus on evaluating images from texture synthesis algorithms.
Compared with synthetic distortion, evaluating images in these
algorithm-based datasets is more complicated, as deformations
introduced by these algorithms do not necessarily lead to perceptual quality degradation. The demand for IQA measures also
extends to specific scenarios, such as screen-content IQA [23],
[24], compressed image IQA [25], [26], the multiview 3D-CG
IQA [27], and light-field IQA [28], [29], posing challenges for
FR-IQA development.

Deep network-based models are also prevalent in current
NR-IQA models, and a key challenge is network training. Approaches such as weakly supervised or self-supervised learning
are commonly used [36], [37], [38], [39]. These strategies train
the network with either pseudo-labeled or unlabeled data to capture robust features independent of subjective evaluation scores.
The trained networks are subsequently used to extract image features and regress the final quality scores. DeepFL [36] trains its
encoder with pseudo-labeled data based on the outputs of several
FR-IQA models, and network training focuses on matching the
FR-IQA score distribution. CONTRIQUE [37] treats network
training as a distortion classification task, aiming to classify
synthetic and authentic distortions. Re-IQA [38] employs a
multiscale feature extraction strategy to train quality-aware and
content-aware encoders. ARNIQA [39] uses a self-supervised
learning approach to maximize the similarity between images
distorted to the same degree, aiming at matching the complex
manifold of diverse distortion types. EffNet-2C-MLSP [40]
aims to evaluate images on a very high-resolution scale and
build NR-IQA models with EfficientNet [18]. These NR-IQA
models all require training an additional score pooling function
to regress subjective scores on testing datasets, which differs
greatly from our proposed model.

B. Deep Network-Based IQA Models
Pioneering deep network-based FR-IQA models such as the
deep similarity index (DeepSIM) [30] use a pretrained VGG
architecture for feature decomposition and the local similarity
index for comparing deep network features. The deep quality
assessment (DeepQA) model [31] applies the VGG-like network
to predict sensitivity maps and regress subjective scores. The
weighted average deep image quality measure (WaDIQaM) [9]
evaluates image quality through patches of deep network features, whereas the learned perceptual image patch similarity
(LPIPS) [32] applies the weighted euclidean distance to compare
deep features from various network architectures. The deep
structure and texture similarity index (DISTS) [1] compares
structural and textural information in VGG network features via
the global similarity index, whereas its enhanced form, adaptive
DISTS (ADISTS) [3], compares deep features via the local
patch-based similarity index. Prashnani et al. [33] modelled the
FR-IQA task as a distance measurement problem, introducing
the perceptual image-error assessment through pairwise preference (PieAPP) [33], which uses a VGG-like architecture and

C. Kernel Representation Similarity Analysis
The self-similarity branch in our dual-branch IQA (DBIQA) is
inspired by representation similarity analysis, a robust analytical
technique that has been applied in various scientific areas, such
as neuroscience [14], natural language processing [41], [42], and
machine learning [15], [43]. It first investigates how information
is organized within a system then compares the collaborative
state of patterns between diverse systems. Technically, the similarity of every two patterns within a system is recorded in a
similarity matrix, offering a holistic view of the overall system.
Differences between these matrices are subsequently measured
to quantify distinctions between systems [14]. The core issue of
the KRSA lies in the measure used to compare the similarity of
two patterns. The basic linear measure, inner products, leads to
a well-known Gram matrix that is effective in tackling texture
synthesis tasks [44] and image retrieval tasks [45]. However,
the HVS is supposed to be a highly nonlinear system, which
means that the simple inner product may not provide perceptual
similarity comparisons. As such, we extend typical RSA into
the kernel method-based form, where the nonlinear kernel is

III. METHODOLOGY
A. Problem Formulation
Given the distorted image X, the reference image Y , and the
deep network φ(• ; θ) with weights θ, the main task in building
deep network-based FR-IQA models can be formalized as
arg max Corr(f (D(φ(X; θ), φ(Y ; θ))), M OS),
{θ,f,D}

(1)

where Corr represents correlation indices such as the Pearson
correlation coefficient (PLCC) and M OS represents the mean
opinion score (MOS). D is the deep feature comparison strategy,
and f is the score pooling function. φ(X; θ) and φ(Y ; θ) are deep
representations of images X and Y , respectively.
Our work focuses on the perceptual comparison strategy D,
so we fix the network φ(• ; θ) as pretrained deep networks on
ImageNet and do not use the MLP. The design of D is based
on two observations. First, in many recent challenging IQA
datasets, the contents of distorted images are usually deformed
and not completely aligned with the reference. Nevertheless, the
HVS can discern appealing deformations. Typical examples in
Fig. 2(a) and (c) illustrate that the HVS sometimes prefers deformations induced by some generative algorithms over typical
distortion types such as JPEG compression or colour diffusion.
However, content deformations do not always result in visual

preference, as shown in Fig. 2(f) and (h), where global or local
content warping leads to perceptual degradation. Specifically,
the HiFiC compression algorithm will generate image content
during compression decoding, and the HVS is less sensitive
to the generated rough image textures shown in Fig. 2(a).
Hence, such deformed content will not lead to drastic perceptual degradation. However, in Fig. 2(c), the HiFiC algorithm
generates a warped human face that leads to drastic perceptual
degradation. Establishing a definitive criterion for how the HVS
discerns appealing deformations may be overcomplicated, but
we find that this property is based on insensitivity to content
misalignment. This idea drives us to use the KRSA because the
comparison of the similarity matrix is less sensitive to content
location variations. The remaining issue is to find a kernel that
can perceptually compare deep network features and discern
appealing deformations. Second, according to the free-energy
principle [46], [47], the HVS can infer pristine images from
distorted images. This property is also mimicked by many NRIQA models [48], [49]. For FR-IQA models, with the presence
of a reference image, this property translates into the models’
differentiability to guide the perceptual image enhancement. The
KRSA comparison alone cannot achieve this objective because
it is insensitive to content locations. Hence, it must be combined
with typical feature pairwise comparisons.

C. Connection to the HVS and Existing Works
Connections to the HVS. The dual-branch framework is
closely related to the HVS. The self-similarity comparison
branch measures the joint degradation of deep network features,
emulating the neuron interaction observed in the HVS. According to Anderson et al. [53], the processing of incoming signals
activates multiple brain regions. When the input changes, the
state of collaborative activations also changes, potentially triggering the brain’s decision-making. Similarly, the self-similarity
branch compares similarity matrices of projected features, emulating the comparison of activation state variations because
the high-dimensional projection divides features into various
subparts, including texture and structure parts, as shown in Fig. 4,
and distortions also change the similarity matrix of these segments. This property is shared by diverse network architectures,
serving as the foundation for the dual-branch framework being
backbone independent. As such, with the learned projection
kernel for the IQA task, the self-similarity comparison branch
can discriminate perceptual-appealing deformations.
Connections to existing models. Existing models typically
conduct pairwise feature comparisons, either in patch form or
attention-weighted form, but do not consider the joint degradation of multiple features. Two works that concern feature
self-similarity comparisons are the deep self-similarity (DSD)

relevance in both quality assessment and image enhancement

C. Quality Prediction Results
1) Results With the VGG Architecture: We present the quality
prediction results of DBIQA with the VGG architecture in
Tables IV and V. According to Table IV, disparities are evident
between the TID2013 [10] and PIPAL training sets [12]. Models
such as WaDIQaM [9] and AHIQ [8] are trained on each of
these datasets, but neither exhibit transferable score prediction
capabilities. In contrast, models such as WaDIQaM [9] and
TOPIQ [34] are trained on the TID2013 [10] and KADID10k [11] datasets and can perform well on each other’s training
sets, indicating that these sets are compatible. Nevertheless, all
these models perform poorly on the SynTEX [22] and TQD [1]
datasets and are inferior on the Li et al. [26] and QADS [21]
datasets, as shown in Table V. This observation suggests that
neither the patch comparison approach nor attention maps can
bridge gaps between synthetic distortion-based datasets and
datasets containing perceptual deformations. This argument is
further demonstrated by DISTS [1] and ADISTS [3]. DISTS [1]

demonstrates robustness in evaluating texture-synthesized images because it compares the global means of VGG features, making it less sensitive to content locations. In contrast,
ADISTS [3] conducts comparisons at the patch level, resulting
in degraded performance on SynTEX [22] and TQD [1] because
the patch-based operation is sensitive to content locations. Moreover, neither DISTS [1] nor ADISTS [3] performs competitively
on TID2013 [10] and the PIPAL training set [12], indicating
that the mean comparison cannot bridge the gap either. Notably,
PieAPP [33] performs competitively across the PIPAL [12]
training, the QADS [21], and several classic IQA datasets, even
though it is blind to them. This observation prompted us to
train DBIQA in the same way as PieAPP [33]. However, the
performance of PieAPP [33] degrades on the PIPAL test set, as
shown in Table VI, possibly because of its patchwise comparison
approach. In conclusion, to enhance the robustness of the FRIQA model, unifying an advanced comparison approach that is
less sensitive to content misalignment with the existing pairwise
feature comparison approach is crucial. By comparing VGG features in the dual-branch framework, our DBIQA model displays
superior performance in Tables IV, V, and VI. It ranks among
the top three best models across 10 out of 11 datasets, which is
unmatched by any other model such as DISTS [1] (3 out of 11),
DeepWSD [6] (3 out of 11), and TOPIQ [34] (5 out of 11) etc..
These results strongly demonstrate that our approach can mimic
HVS behaviours in broader scenarios, which is attributed
mainly to the combination of two powerful feature comparison
strategies.

2) Results With Diverse Network Architectures: Various network architectures, including ResNet [4], SqueezeNet [16],
MobileNet [17], and EfficientNet [18], have superior score
prediction capabilities within dual-branch frameworks. The results in Tables VI and VII demonstrate their high perceptual
relevance, which can be attributed to three reasons. First, pretrained networks can capture perceptual features that are vital
in perceptual quality evaluations. For example, the shallow
layers of convolutional networks typically capture low-level
perceptual features such as image outlines and structures, which
are also vulnerable to distortions. Second, convolutional neural
networks share a common property: deeper stage features have
broader receptive fields, whereas shallow stage features have
restricted receptive fields. Comparing features from shallow to
deeper stages mimics the multiscale evaluation mechanism in
HVS, which is also demonstrated in many IQA works [20],
[60]. Third, feature self-similarity is prevalent in these network
features. The dual-branch framework enables perceptual comparison by measuring joint degradation among deep network
features, thereby demonstrating independence from the specific
network backbones.
Beyond backbone independence, two important findings must
be clarified. First, the quality assessment results are independent of the network complexity and classification accuracy.
For example, SqueezeNet [16] and MobileNet [17] are networks with lighter structures and lower classification accuracies
than ResNet [4]. Nevertheless, DBIQA with these two network
architectures can yield higher PLCC and SRCC results than
ResNet [4] on TID2013 [10], CSIQ [20], and the PIPAL [12]
training sets, as shown in Table VII. These results also indicate

that features that lead to higher classification accuracy may not
absolutely benefit the quality assessment task. Tailoring these
network features for IQA is necessary. Second, the success of
the dual-branch framework cannot be attributed to the similarity
matrix encoding texture or style information. As indicated by
Wang et al. [66], the deep feature similarity matrix of networks
other than VGG does not have a specific perceptual meaning,
and the Gram matrix of the ResNet feature [4] cannot address
texture synthesis or the style transfer task. The core reason is
the insensitivity to content location variations. Robustness to
perceptual deformations can be realized only based on such
properties. As such, by incorporating the kernel method-based
projection, our proposed DBIQA can adapt well to diverse
network architectures.

V. CONCLUSION
We introduce a dual-branch framework to unify feature selfsimilarity comparisons and feature pairwise comparisons, aiming to explore the joint degradation effect in discerning appealing
content deformations. Our proposed feature comparison approach is robust to perceptual deformations across diverse IQA
scenarios and can also guide perceptual image enhancement,
unifying the perceptual quality assessment and enhancement.
This issue is an underexplored part of current IQA research.
Furthermore, our approach is backbone-independent. We tested
various deep neural networks, all delivering perceptual quality
assessment results. These findings demonstrate two important
things. First, content deformation issues widely exist in modern
IQA scenarios, and measuring the joint degradation effect can
better interpret these complex distortion types. Second, unifying
feature self-similarity and feature pairwise comparisons are
